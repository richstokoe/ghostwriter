# Voice & Style

The story is *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*. Its telling is warm, playful, and unmistakably Victorian, following Alice in close third-person while slipping freely into her own thoughts.

- **Close third-person, free and indirect.** The narration keeps to Alice's point of view and often borrows her exact wording, then drops into her thoughts in quotation marks: "and what is the use of a book," thought Alice, "without pictures or conversations?"
- **Long, winding sentences.** Clauses gather one after another, linked by commas, semicolons, and "and", so a single breath can carry Alice from the riverbank to the bottom of the rabbit-hole. Let a sentence run when the thought runs.
- **Asides in parentheses.** The narrator leans in to comment, often with a wink: "(Which was very likely true.)" Keep them light and quick.
- **Speak to the reader.** A gentle "you see" or "you know" draws the reader into the joke without breaking the spell.
- **Nonsense played straight.** Impossible things are told in the earnest, reasonable tone of a child reasoning them out. The sillier the premise, the more matter-of-fact the telling.
- **Curiosity is the engine.** Alice wonders, questions, and tries things; what she feels shows in what she does and says, never in a named emotion.
- **Emphasis by italics and repetition.** A stressed word takes italics (*very*, *never*); rhythm comes from plain repetition ("Down, down, down.").
- **Period detail, kept concrete.** Marmalade jars, waistcoat-pockets, daisy-chains, and lessons in the schoolroom: real Victorian things, named plainly and left unexplained.

The built-in house-style checks still apply: favour the concrete word, and let punctuation stay simple so the voice carries the strangeness.
